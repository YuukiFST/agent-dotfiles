01 · the whole idea

Two lanes, one map

Every big project is a pile of open questions. Helmsman splits that pile in two — and only one half is yours.

Questions about what the system does are yours.
Questions about how it gets built are the agent's.

03 · the exceptions

The three things that come back to you

Technical decisions whose consequence lands on you rather than on the code. These three, and nothing else.

Money

Recurring spend, a paid tier, or a setup fee. A free tier the project will plausibly blow through counts too.

E.g. "speech recognition can run on a paid API ($35/month, works today) or on a local model (free, slower, more work)."

Personal data

Personal or sensitive data leaving your control: sent to a third party, stored in another country, or kept longer than the product needs it.

E.g. "ticket text passes through a third-party server to be classified. Do you want that, or a dumber classifier running on your own machine?"

Lock-in

A choice you can only undo by rewriting, not by swapping. Vendor, hosting platform, or the data model everything else grows into.

E.g. "if the data lives in Firebase, leaving later means rewriting the whole backend. The alternative costs two extra weeks now."

And it arrives in this shape — never as a menu of five options

◆ ticket for you · escalation

What I need from you
Turning the recorded audio into text costs money every month, or it costs speed. I need your go-ahead.
My recommendation
The paid API, ~$35/month at your volume. Transcription lands in seconds and the feature ships on day one.
The alternative
A model running on your own machine: no monthly cost, but ~4 minutes per recording and two more weeks of work.
Why this one is yours
Money — it is recurring spend out of your pocket, not an engineering preference.

The answer it expects is "go" or "no, because —". That's all.

04 · the artifact

Anatomy of the map

The map is a single issue. It is the only thing you ever open — and it is written to be read by you, not by a programmer.

1## Destination

A booking system where the client books alone and the
salon stops answering the phone to confirm times.

2## Notes

Domain: hair salon, 3 stylists.
Every session consults /grilling and /domain-modeling.

3## Product decisions

- [Can a client reschedule alone?](#) — yes, up to 4h before
- [What happens on a no-show?](#) — blocked for 30 days

4## Technical decisions

- [Where bookings are stored](#) — Postgres —
  locks: a slot is an interval, not a point in time
- [How a client identifies themselves](#) — magic link by
  email — locks: no password screen anywhere

5## Waiting on you

- [Can a stylist refuse a client?](#) — may they turn down
  a booking that already came in?

6## Not yet specified

- How the salon notifies the client (SMS? email? both?)
  — hangs on how the client identifies themselves

7## Out of scope

- [Prepayment](#) — waits until the first month is
  running
  1. Destination. Where this map ends. It is the first thing you settle and the thing that fixes scope — everything past it is "out of scope".
  2. Notes. Domain, preferences that hold for the whole effort, skills every session should consult.
  3. Product decisions. What you answered, in your own language. One line per closed question.
  4. Technical decisions. What it decided. The locks: clause is not decoration: it is what the next session reads so it doesn't contradict itself.
  5. Waiting on you. The open questions that are yours. If this list is empty, the agent still has work it can do alone.
  6. Not yet specified. The fog: what you can tell is coming but can't phrase sharply yet.
  7. Out of scope. What was consciously cut. It never comes back — unless you redraw the destination.

05 · the engine

The life of a question

Nothing is born as a ticket. It starts as fog, becomes a question once it can be phrased, and answering it opens the fog behind it.

06 · using it

Three commands, and only one costs you time

/helmsman <your idea>

1. Chart the map

The session where you do the talking. It interviews you until it understands the whole product, creates the map and fills it with tickets. It asks you no technical question here — those become its own tickets.

Ends when the map exists and you know how many questions are yours.

your time: ~40 min
/helmsman <map>

2. Advance alone

It resolves a technical ticket, re-reads the fog, takes the next one, repeats. Hit an escalation and it files the ticket for you and keeps going on the rest — one money question does not idle the session.

Ends when only your work is left, or context runs out.

your time: zero
/helmsman <map> --review

3. Answer what's yours

Drains every accumulated question in one conversation, instead of forcing a session per question. Each answer is recorded as it lands — if the conversation gets cut short, nothing is lost.

Ends when the "Waiting on you" list hits zero.

your time: ~20 min

You don't have to remember which of the three comes next

Every session — all three of them — ends by handing you the next command, ready to run, with the map's link already inside. You copy it, paste it into the next session, done. Picking which one is its job, not yours:

/helmsman https://github.com/salon/booking/issues/1

your time: zero — it resumes the cascade at the ticket "How the salon notifies the client".

The normal rhythm is 2 → 3 → 2 → 3 until no decision is left. You start each of those laps with a copy and paste. Then the last command handed over is /writing-plans against the map, and building starts with everything already decided.

07 · in practice

One full lap

The same project, crossing all three modes.

session 1 · you talk

/helmsman "I want a booking system for the salon"

agentWhen a client doesn't show up and didn't warn anyone, what should happen?
youThey're blocked for 30 days. I've lost too much money on that.
agentAnd rescheduling? How close to the appointment can they still move it themselves?
youUp to 4 hours before. After that they have to call.

Not one technical question came up. They became its tickets:

  • Map created, destination and scope settled — 14 tickets
  • 5 are yours — rules only you know
  • 9 are its own — storage, identity, notifications, deploy…

It closes by handing you the next command:

/helmsman https://github.com/salon/booking/issues/1

session 2 · you're not there

/helmsman <map> — cascade

  • Where bookings are stored → Postgres. Alternatives: SQLite (rejected: two stylists writing at once), Firebase (rejected: lock-in with no gain here). Criterion: "a slot is an interval, not a point" — needs exclusion over ranges, which Postgres does natively.
  • How a client identifies themselves → magic link by email. Criterion anchored to your decision "the client installs nothing". Locks: no password screen anywhere in the system.
  • How the salon notifies the client → became your question. SMS costs $0.03 a message. Recurring spend = escalation. It filed the ticket and moved on.
  • + 4 more technical decisions, each recorded with its alternatives and criterion

It stopped because the technical frontier emptied. Six questions are yours — so the command it hands over is the other one:

/helmsman https://github.com/salon/booking/issues/1 --review

session 3 · 20 minutes of yours

/helmsman <map> --review

  • All six questions in one conversation — not six sessions
  • Including the SMS one: "I recommend email, $0/month, and SMS only for the 24h reminder if you want it later" → you approved
  • Every answer recorded as it landed, "Waiting on you" hit zero

"That unblocks 5 technical questions, and I'll work through all of them alone":

/helmsman https://github.com/salon/booking/issues/1

You answered what the system does. The block you copy sends the how to a session that doesn't need you.

08 · choosing

helmsman or wayfinder?

Same map, same ticket mechanics. Exactly one thing changes: who answers the technical question.

When this comes up…/wayfinder/helmsman
"what does the system do?"youyou
"which database?"youthe agent
"this will cost $35/month"youyou
"how much of the project do we cut?"youyou
Sessions that need younearly all1 in 3

Use wayfinder when you want to make the technical calls yourself.
Use helmsman when your job is saying what the system has to be — and its job is working out how.
